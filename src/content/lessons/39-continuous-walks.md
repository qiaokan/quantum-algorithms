## Problem and access model

A continuous-time walk is the evolution $e^{-itA}$ generated by a graph's adjacency matrix $A$: $A_{jk}=1$ when vertices $j,k$ are connected, and zero otherwise. Here $t$ is the chosen evolution time, $i^2=-1$, and the matrix exponential is defined below. This is a simulation primitive. It produces a vertex sample, rather than a classical list of all paths. [Farhi and Gutmann](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9706062) introduced a continuous-time approach to computation on graphs; their early examples also illustrate why comparison with one particular classical walk is insufficient to prove a general classical lower bound.

## Classical baseline and a two-vertex instance

Take vertices 0 and 1 joined by one edge:

$$A=X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad |\psi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle=\binom10.$$

A classical continuous-time symmetric random walk flips at random waiting times. Starting at 0, its probability of vertex 1 is $(1-e^{-2t})/2$, approaching 1/2. The quantum evolution below can reach vertex 1 with certainty. This two-state difference is not a speedup claim: either distribution is trivial to compute classically.

## Detour: what does a matrix exponential mean?

For a number $z$, $e^z=1+z+z^2/2!+z^3/3!+\cdots$; $3!=3\cdot2\cdot1$. Use the same convergent series with a square matrix. Here $X^2=I$ because exchanging two coordinates twice restores them. Consequently even powers of $X$ are $I$ and odd powers are $X$. Grouping terms gives

$$\begin{aligned}
e^{-itX}
&=I-itX-\tfrac{t^2}{2}I+\tfrac{it^3}{6}X+\cdots\\
&=(1-t^2/2!+t^4/4!-\cdots)I-i(t-t^3/3!+\cdots)X\\
&=\cos(t)I-i\sin(t)X.
\end{aligned}$$

Sine and cosine give the vertical and horizontal coordinates of a point at angle $t$ on a unit circle; in particular $\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1$. We needed the exponential to calculate the actual walk. We can now return to applying it to $|0\rangle$.

## Evolve, measure, and verify

$$|\psi(t)\rangle=
\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&-i\sin t\\-i\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix}\binom10
=\binom{\cos t\cdot1-i\sin t\cdot0}{-i\sin t\cdot1+\cos t\cdot0}
=\cos t|0\rangle-i\sin t|1\rangle.$$

At $t=\pi/4$, amplitudes are $1/\sqrt2,-i/\sqrt2$; probabilities are both 1/2. At $t=\pi/2$, the state is $-i|1\rangle$, so measuring position gives 1 with certainty. The factor $-i$ is then a [global phase](/definitions/phase/). At $t=\pi$ the state is $-|0\rangle$: the walker returns. Probability is conserved, not diffused irreversibly toward a stationary distribution.

## Check the eigenvector interpretation numerically

For $|+\rangle=(1,1)^T/\sqrt2$, multiplication by X yields the same vector: its eigenvalue is +1. For $|-\rangle=(1,-1)^T/\sqrt2$, X gives $(-1,1)^T/\sqrt2=-|-\rangle$, so its eigenvalue is −1. These two normalized vectors have inner product $(1-1)/2=0$. They form an orthonormal basis.

Starting from $|0\rangle=(|+\rangle+|-\rangle)/\sqrt2$, evolve the two components separately. At $t=\pi/4$, their phase factors are $(1-i)/\sqrt2$ and $(1+i)/\sqrt2$. Converting back to the original basis, the amplitude of |0〉 is their sum divided by two, $1/\sqrt2$; the amplitude of |1〉 is their difference divided by two, $-i/\sqrt2$. This reproduces the direct matrix calculation. We needed eigenvectors to find directions that only rotate in phase, then returned to the position basis where those phases interfere.

## How to implement the evolution

On one qubit use $R_x(2t)=e^{-itX}$. The rotation angle is $2t$, not $t$, under the common convention $R_x(\theta)=e^{-i\theta X/2}$. This is a mathematical convention, not a library call. On a general graph, one must construct Hamiltonian simulation from its edge descriptions or sparse-neighbor oracle. [Product formulas](/lessons/product-formulas/), [qubitization](/lessons/qubitization/), and [QSVT](/lessons/singular-value-transformation/) supply different implementations and error bounds. An adjacency matrix with exponentially many explicitly supplied entries is not a free input.

## General behavior and boundaries

If $A|v_j\rangle=\lambda_j|v_j\rangle$, where $|v_j\rangle$ is an [eigenvector](/definitions/eigenvalues/), evolution multiplies that component by $e^{-it\lambda_j}$. For our edge, $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ have eigenvalues +1 and −1. Since $|0\rangle=(|+\rangle+|-\rangle)/\sqrt2$, their relative phase produces the transfer we calculated. This connects directly to phase estimation: the energy or graph eigenvalue becomes an observable phase after a chosen time.

Adjacency and graph-Laplacian walks are different conventions. On a regular graph they differ by a constant multiple of identity and hence by a global phase (and possibly time/sign convention); on an irregular graph they need not have the same position probabilities. Do not change between them silently. Physical implementation also needs coherence over the required evolution and gate-approximation error under control. No hardware experiment is reported here.

## Reproduce the calculation

The [downloadable NumPy checks](/examples/applications.py) contain independent small-matrix calculations for this part of the book. Save the file locally and run `python3 applications.py` in an environment with NumPy installed. The checks compare computed amplitudes, probabilities, matrix identities, or estimator moments against the worked results; they do not submit circuits to hardware. Conditional states are normalized only after their success probability is computed. The code labels the register ordering and reports failed assertions rather than silently repairing states.
