## What the framework asks for

A group is a set with an operation, identity and inverses. It is **abelian** when changing operand order leaves the result unchanged. Integers modulo 8 under addition are an example: $6+5\equiv3\pmod8$, and the inverse of 6 is 2. A **subgroup** is a subset that is itself closed under the same operation and inverses. The subset $H=\{0,4\}$ is a subgroup of the integers modulo 8.

A **coset** $a+H$ is the subgroup shifted by a known or unknown group element $a$. For $a=1$, it is $\{1,5\}$. A function hides $H$ if it takes the same value on each coset and different values on different cosets. Given coherent evaluation of that function, recover generators for $H$—a short list whose repeated combinations produce all of it. This is a framework, not an assertion that every symmetry problem is efficiently solved. [Childs and van Dam](https://arxiv.org/html/0812.0380) explain the abelian Fourier-sampling construction and its boundaries.

## Why ordinary queries miss the structure

Classically, a repeated function value gives evidence that the difference of two queried inputs belongs to $H$. A sufficiently large table can reveal the subgroup, but its cost depends on the group and promise. Simon's specific black-box family has an exponential randomized query separation. Modular arithmetic problems instead supply structured circuits, and their classical hardness is not proved by that oracle separation. Keep those statements separate.

The quantum plan prepares one random coset coherently, Fourier-transforms it, and measures constraints that all hidden subgroup elements satisfy.

## Work one complete example

Let the known group be integers modulo 8 and let the hidden function be $f(x)=x\bmod4$, provided as an oracle. The learner can inspect this formula to verify the example, but an oracle algorithm is charged for queries. Its table is $0,1,2,3,0,1,2,3$.

Prepare $\sum_{x=0}^7|x\rangle|0\rangle/\sqrt8$ and compute $f(x)$. Measuring output value 1 occurs with probability $2/8=1/4$, leaving

$$|\psi\rangle={|1\rangle+|5\rangle\over\sqrt2}.$$

Apply $F_8$. Output label $y$ has amplitude

$$b_y={e^{2\pi iy/8}+e^{2\pi i5y/8}\over4}
={e^{2\pi iy/8}\over4}(1+e^{\pi iy}).$$

For odd $y$, $e^{\pi iy}=-1$ and cancellation gives zero. For even $y$, it is 1, so the amplitude has magnitude $1/2$. Labels 0,2,4,6 therefore each occur with probability $1/4$.

Suppose $y=2$. This measurement tells us that every hidden $h\in H$ satisfies

$$e^{2\pi i(2h)/8}=1.$$

A phase equals 1 when it makes a whole number of turns, so $2h$ must be divisible by 8. The possible $h$ values modulo 8 are 0 and 4. This yields the candidate $H=\{0,4\}$, generated by 4. One sample does not certify that candidate: a smaller hidden subgroup could also produce it. For this eight-input teaching table, checking that $f(0)=f(4),f(1)=f(5),f(2)=f(6),f(3)=f(7)$ and that the four values differ verifies the candidate directly, at the cost of inspecting the tiny table. Large instances instead collect enough independent samples for a quantified confidence guarantee. If we observe 4, it only constrains $h$ to be even. Outcome 0 imposes no constraint.

## Characters: a name for the phase patterns

A **character** is a phase-valued function that turns the group operation into multiplication: $\chi(x+x')=\chi(x)\chi(x')$. The prime in $x'$ means “another input,” not a derivative. For our group, $\chi_y(x)=e^{2\pi iyx/8}$ is a character. For example, with $y=2$, $\chi_2(1)=i$ and $\chi_2(2)=-1=i\cdot i$.

We needed this definition to express what the Fourier measurement tests. Summing a character over a subgroup gives either reinforcement, when it equals 1 everywhere on that subgroup, or cancellation. To see cancellation, let $S=\sum_{h\in H}\chi(h)$ and choose $h_0\in H$ with $\chi(h_0)\ne1$. Shifting by $h_0$ merely reorders $H$, so $S=\chi(h_0)S$. Subtracting gives $(1-\chi(h_0))S=0$, hence $S=0$. This is the general version of $1+(-1)=0$ above.

The surviving character labels form the **annihilator** of $H$, meaning those characters equal to 1 on every element of $H$. In our example it is $\{0,2,4,6\}$. The name is optional; the cancellation calculation is essential.

## Reconstruction, cost and assumptions

Repeated Fourier samples generate enough independent constraints to identify the subgroup. When a finite abelian group's decomposition into cyclic factors and efficient group operations are available, its Fourier transform factors into manageable cyclic transforms; classical modular linear algebra reconstructs generators. Both the group representation and the postprocessing must be efficient. A generic statement “take the QFT over the group” is not an implementation.

Each trial includes uniform group-state preparation, coherent function evaluation, Fourier transformation and measurement. Function construction may dominate runtime. Noise, approximate transforms, probability of independent samples and classical arithmetic affect repetitions and precision. No individual trial deterministically reveals the subgroup in general.

## Return to the preceding algorithms

For [Simon](/lessons/simon/), the group operation is bitwise XOR, the hidden subgroup is $\{0,s\}$, and the character is $(-1)^{y\cdot x}$. The surviving equation is $y\cdot s=0\bmod2$.

For [discrete logarithms](/lessons/discrete-logarithms/), the subgroup is the modular line $\{(sy,y)\}$, and the surviving condition is $us+v=0\bmod r$. Our concrete $r=8$ example recovered $s=3$ from $(u,v)=(1,5)$.

For [order finding](/lessons/order-finding/), the period lives in the integers; a finite window approximates its Fourier information and continued fractions recover a denominator. That finite-window approximation is why the order-finding lesson had precision and unsuccessful samples, rather than exact finite-group cancellation in every case.

The abelian success does not automatically extend to nonabelian hidden-subgroup problems. Dihedral hidden shifts require additional ideas, and other nonabelian cases remain difficult. The shared vocabulary organizes known mechanisms; it is not a universal algorithm generator.

## Reproduce the calculation

Download the [standalone numerical checks](/examples/search-fourier-checks.py) and run `python3 search-fourier-checks.py`. They use only Python’s standard library, construct the relevant vectors and transformations, and check normalization without silently renormalizing. They test the tiny ideal examples; they are not hardware runs or scalable implementations of every general algorithm.
